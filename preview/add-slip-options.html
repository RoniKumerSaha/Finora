The add screens, round two — one slip you write on

The first attempt put a form on the left and a live certificate on the right. Two documents, and to check a number you had to look across the gap and back. It read as a web form with a receipt preview stapled to it.

This round takes one position: the record is the page. There is no form and no preview — there is ONE document, drawn at full width, with its blanks still open. You fill it in place. The consequence ("Cash goes from ৳ 53,500 to ৳ 51,000") is a clause of the document, not a toast bolted under it.

Three consequences of that position, applied identically to all five screens: the amount is the headline (the loudest thing on the page, because it is the one thing you always know); the one big decision is a stamp you press, not a dropdown that hides the alternative; and the lines you fill are blanks — the label engraved, the rule waiting for your ink.

What changed
The old add form had three cards to choose from and a separate preview card on the right. You picked a type, the screen re-rendered a different form, and the effect of your entry appeared in a banner at the very bottom — below the fold, in the case of most phones.

Here the direction is the first stamp on the slip, the amount is the headline, and the consequence sits in the same sheet, four lines below the fields that caused it.
Why a stamp and not a dropdown
"Money went out" and "moved between accounts" get confused more than any other pair — an ATM withdrawal looks like an expense until you remember it's your own money walking to another account. A dropdown hides that alternative behind a closed list. Three stamps show all three options, side by side, every time.

The smallest form, the most ceremony
An account is three fields. But it is the root of the ledger — every transaction points at one, and an account opened with the wrong type wears the wrong icon for the rest of your life. So the type gets four big stamps, and the opening balance gets the same headline treatment as a transaction amount, because it's the same kind of fact: a quantity of money.

The clause earns its place
On the other four screens the clause just restates a balance. Here it does arithmetic: it tells you whether the number you typed and the date you picked actually meet. That's the one question a goal screen can answer that a note field can't — so it's the one screen where the clause is the point rather than a reassurance.

The direction flip is the point
The old form used a dropdown, so switching between "I owe" and "owed to me" changed a label but left the whole rest of the form ambiguous — which account, which number, all of it read as if it were still an expense. Two big stamps, each labelled in plain words — "You borrowed" / "You lent" — remove the ambiguity entirely, because you can see both options at the moment you're choosing.
What every screen shares
One slip · a stamp for the decision · the amount as the headline · engraved blanks · a clause with the consequence · a foot with the save.

Learn it once on transactions, and accounts, goals, investments and debts are already familiar.
